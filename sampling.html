Part 1
Pure theory

Not one line of source code in this part. Everything the sampler is built on, from zero: what a sampling loop is for, how the fifty moments of a generation are chosen and why they are not evenly spaced, the one line of arithmetic that turns fifty answers into a picture, how every token gets a four-number address, what happens to a photograph before it is allowed near the network, the two completely different meanings of "guidance", and what the loop does differently on its first step when a KV cache is involved. Read this once and every listing in Parts 3 and 4 is a recognition rather than a discovery.

§ 1The loop around the network

A FLUX.2 generation calls the transformer once per step — four times for a klein model, fifty times for flux.2-dev. model.py is what happens inside one of those calls. This file is what happens between them: it picks the times, prepares everything that is not noise, adds the answers up, and hands the result back as something a VAE can decode. It has no weights, no classes and no training code — just 442 lines of bookkeeping that decide what the picture looks like.

1.1 · The three jobs

Everything in sampling.py does one of three things. Naming them now is worth it, because the rest of this article is those three names in order:

JobWhat it meansWhere it lives
1 · Choose the times A generation walks from t = 1 (pure noise) to t = 0 (image). It cannot visit every t, so it visits N+1 of them. Which ones? Not evenly spaced ones — the ladder is deliberately bent toward the noisy end, and how far it is bent depends on the resolution and on how many steps you can afford. L240–266 · three functions
2 · Prepare the inputs The network takes tokens, and tokens need addresses. Text arrives as rows from a language model, the canvas starts as Gaussian noise, and reference photographs arrive as JPEGs that must be colour-converted, size-capped, cropped to a multiple of 16, normalised to [−1, 1], encoded and then given coordinates that keep them distinguishable from the canvas. On the way out, the flat token list has to become a picture again. L12–237 · fifteen functions
3 · Run the loop Call the network, take one step, repeat. Three variants exist, and they differ in how many batches they push through and whether the reference tokens are recomputed every step or paid for once. L269–410 · three functions

Jobs 1 and 2 are preparation: they run once, before the first call, and their output never changes during a generation. Job 3 is the only part that repeats — and inside it, the only thing that changes from one iteration to the next is the image itself and the single float that says how noisy it currently is.

1.2 · One generation, end to end

Here is a whole FLUX.2 generation as the command-line script actually performs it, with the file that owns each step written on the right. Nine of the eleven steps are this file's work; the two that are not are the transformer call and the two autoencoder calls.

▸ Why is the noise drawn as 128 × H/16 × W/16?

FLUX.2 never generates pixels directly. The autoencoder compresses a picture 8× in each spatial direction and the patchifier folds 2×2 latent cells into one token, so a 1024×1024 image becomes 64×64 = 4 096 tokens of 128 numbers each. The loop therefore works on a tensor that is already "one row per token", and the file's first job is to remember where each row came from — which is what the ids in x_ids are for (§4).

1.3 · What is deliberately not in this file

Knowing what the sampler refuses to do explains most of its shape:

AbsentWhat that implies
No weights, no nn.Module Not one line here is trained. The file is pure orchestration, which is why it can afford to be plain Python: loops over lists, torch.cat, arithmetic on floats.
No classes at all Twenty-one top-level functions and nothing else. There is no sampler object to configure, no state between calls — the state of a generation is exactly two tensors (img and the loop counter) plus an optional cache dictionary.
No scheduler library No diffusers, no DDIMScheduler, no config files. The whole schedule is three functions and eleven numeric constants (§12).
No higher-order solver No momentum, no second-order correction, no solver state. One prediction, one step, added to the image. Rectified-flow paths are straight enough that plain Euler is what the models were distilled against (§3).
No stochasticity in the loop After the initial randn, nothing is random. The same seed, prompt and step count give the same image, bit for bit.
✦ The one-sentence version of this file

Turn everything into addressed tokens, choose a bent ladder of times, then repeatedly ask the network which way is real and walk that way. Every function in the remaining 439 lines is one of those four verbs.

§ 2The schedule: which times to ask about

A generation cannot visit every moment between pure noise and a finished image — it can afford four visits, or fifty. Which four? FLUX.2's answer is the most consequential number-crunching in this file: three small functions, eleven hard-coded constants, and a ladder of times that is deliberately, strongly uneven.

2.1 · The obvious ladder, and why it is not used

The obvious choice is evenly spaced: eight steps means t = 1.000, 0.875, 0.750, 0.625, 0.500, 0.375, 0.250, 0.125, and then 0. Every step covers exactly one eighth of the way. FLUX.2 does not do this. With the same eight steps, at 1024×1024, it asks about:

t = 1.000, 0.985, 0.967, 0.942, 0.906, 0.853, 0.763, 0.580 → 0

Four of the eight calls happen while the image is still more than 94 % noise, and the very last step crosses 58 % of the entire range in one go — from t = 0.580 straight to t = 0. That looks wasteful until you notice what the two schedules are spending their steps on. The even ladder spends half its calls in the late phase, where the picture is already formed and each call only refines texture. The bent ladder spends nearly all of them in the early phase, where composition, lighting and shapes are decided — and then trusts one long stride to finish the job.

2.2 · Bending the ladder: one number, applied to all of it

The bending is done by a single formula, and it is worth understanding it exactly, because it is the only place where "how many steps" and "how big the image" reach the pixels. Take any time t between 0 and 1 and map it to a new time t′:

t′ = eμeμ + (1t − 1)σ with σ = 1 in FLUX.2, so the exponent does nothing

Read literally, this is a signal-to-noise ratio being rescaled: (1/t − 1) is the ratio of noise to signal at time t, and dividing it by eμ makes every moment look noisier than it was. But there is a much better way to see it.

∑ The shift is a slide along the logit axis

Define the logit of a time as logit(t) = ln(t / (1 − t)) — the logarithm of the signal-to-noise ratio. It runs from −∞ at t = 0 through 0 at t = 0.5 to +∞ at t = 1. Now rewrite the formula with σ = 1:

t′ = eμ·teμ·t + (1 − t) ⇒ t′1 − t′ = eμ·t1 − t ⇒ logit(t′) = logit(t) + μ

That is the whole trick: the shift adds a constant to the logit of every timestep. With the general σ it is the affine map logit(t′) = σ·logit(t) + μ — a stretch and a slide. The two ends are fixed points (±∞ + μ = ±∞), which is why t = 1 and t = 0 survive any shift, and why μ can be large without breaking the ladder. VERIFIED to 10−12 at sixteen (t, μ, σ) combinations by src/ref/verify_sampling.py.

▸ What "noisier" buys you

The published reason for shifting is resolution. At a fixed t, a bigger image hides its noise better: with four times as many latent cells, averaging over neighbours recovers more of the original signal, so the same t is an easier problem. If you keep the ladder fixed while the canvas grows, the model spends its steps on questions it has already answered. Shifting by a larger μ at larger resolutions restores the difficulty — which is exactly the dependence on image_seq_len that compute_empirical_mu implements (§2.3). This argument and the shift itself come from the Stable Diffusion 3 paper (Esser et al., 2024, §5.2).

2.3 · Where the bend comes from: two fitted lines

So the shift is one number μ. Where does μ come from? Not from a config file and not from a formula derived from first principles — from two straight lines fitted to measurements, plus a linear interpolation between them. Call L the number of image tokens in the canvas (4 096 at 1024×1024) and N the number of steps:

m10(L) = 8.73809524·10−5·L + 1.89833333 the μ that suits a 10-step run
m200(L) = 1.6927·10−4·L + 0.45666666 the μ that suits a 200-step run

For a canvas of L ≤ 4 300 tokens the code interpolates between those two anchors on the step count, and it does it so that both ends come out exactly right: with a = (m₂₀₀ − m₁₀) / 190 and b = m₂₀₀ − 200a, the value μ = a·N + b equals m10 at N = 10 and m200 at N = 200. For a canvas above 4 300 tokens it skips the interpolation entirely and returns m200(L), whatever the step count.

Two things about this are worth stopping for.

Fewer steps means a stronger shift. Both fitted lines rise with L, but the 10-step line rises more slowly and starts higher, so in the whole supported range m10 > m200 and the slope a = (m₂₀₀ − m₁₀)/190 is negative: μ falls as the step count grows. A model with only four chances puts them all high up in the noise; a model with fifty can afford to spend the last twenty on detail. (The two lines would only cross at L ≈ 17 605 tokens, well past the 16 384 tokens of a 4-megapixel canvas, so this ordering never reverses in practice.)

⚠ The schedule is not continuous in resolution

At the knee, μ jumps. Ask for a 1040×1040 image and the canvas is 65×65 = 4 225 tokens, so a 4-step run interpolates and gets μ = 2.3021. Ask for 1056×1056 — 1.5 % more pixels — and the canvas is 66×66 = 4 356 tokens, past the knee, so the same 4-step run gets μ = 1.1940. The bend is nearly halved by a rounding boundary, and the ladder changes shape with it — [1.000, 0.908, 0.767, 0.524, 0] instead of [1.000, 0.968, 0.909, 0.769, 0], i.e. the model is suddenly asked about t = 0.524 where a moment ago it was asked about 0.769. Nothing in the file guards against this; a 50-step run over the same two sizes moves μ from 2.0368 to 1.1940 and its final stride from 0.135 to 0.063. VERIFIED by calling compute_empirical_mu and get_schedule at 4 225 and 4 356 tokens.

✦ What the constants were fitted to inference

The function is named compute_empirical_mu and its eight decimals are unmistakably measured rather than derived, but the repository does not say what was measured. The shape of the two lines suggests a sweep over canvas sizes and step counts, scoring the samples and keeping the μ that looked best at each end of the step range — the same recipe FLUX.1 used, where μ was interpolated between two sequence lengths. The knee at 4 300 tokens then reads as "above roughly one megapixel, the step count stopped mattering in the measurements". Treat that reading as a guess; the numbers themselves are not guesses.

Read down the last column and three habits of this sampler become visible.

The first call is always at exactly t = 1. The ladder starts at pure noise and the shift leaves 1 alone, so the network's first question is always "here is Gaussian noise, which way?" — a case it was trained on.

The model is never called at t = 0. The zero at the end of every ladder is a destination, not a query: the loop pairs each time with the next one and calls the network at the first of the pair, so the last entry is only ever used as a stride length. This is why the final step is so long, and why "asking the model what a finished image looks like" never happens.

Resolution moves the whole ladder, not just its length. At 50 steps and 1024² the model gets 39 of its 50 calls above t = 0.7; at 2048² it gets 46. The last stride grows from 0.1337 to 0.3403 — the higher the resolution, the more of the descent is left to one final jump.

✦ Reference images do not change the schedule

The step count and the canvas size are the only inputs to get_schedule, and the canvas size it is given is the token count of the noise — four reference photographs add 16 384 tokens to every call and leave the ladder untouched. The schedule is chosen by the picture you want, never by the pictures you brought along.

§ 3One line of arithmetic: the Euler step

Fifty network calls produce fifty predictions. The image is what you get by adding them up — and "adding them up" is a single line, repeated verbatim in all three loops of this file. It is the only place where the schedule of §2 touches a pixel.

img = img + (t_prev − t_curr) · pred (t_prev − t_curr) < 0, always

Three facts make this line readable. First, pred is the network's estimate of the velocity — the direction from the clean image toward the noise, v ≈ x₁ − x₀, which is what a rectified-flow model is trained to output. Second, the ladder of §2 descends, so t_prev − t_curr is negative and the product points against the velocity: toward the image. Third, the size of that negative number is the stride, and it is the whole contribution of the schedule.

◆ Why one step would be enough on a straight path

Suppose the path really is the straight line x(t) = (1−t)·x₀ + t·x₁, so the velocity is the constant v = x₁ − x₀. Start at noise, t = 1, and take one step with t_prev − t_curr = 0 − 1 = −1:

x1 + (−1)·(x1 − x0) = x1 − x1 + x0 = x0

Exactly the image, in one call. Every extra step exists for one reason only: the learned velocity field is not perfectly straight and not perfectly predicted, so the sampler re-asks along the way and corrects. This is also why the strides of §2 must sum to −1 — the loop has to travel the whole path, no more. VERIFIED: for the 4-, 8-, 28- and 50-step ladders the sum of t_prev − t_curr is −1.0000000 in every case.

3.1 · The same line, three times

There is no solver abstraction, no step function, no scheduler object. Each of the three loops ends with its own copy of the update, written out in full:

LoopLineWhat surrounds it
denoise305 img = img + (t_prev - t_curr) * pred — after one call that may have carried reference tokens, whose predictions were just thrown away (§14).
denoise_cached353 The same line, reached either from forward_kv_extract (step 0) or forward_kv_cached (every step after) — the arithmetic does not care which (§15).
denoise_cfg408 The same line, but pred has already been combined from two halves of a doubled batch (§16).

That triplication is a deliberate trade: the three loops differ in what they feed the model and what they do with the answer, and each is short enough to read top to bottom in thirty seconds. There is no shared base class to go and look at.

3.2 · The time the model is told, and the time the model receives

One detail of this line's neighbourhood matters more than it looks. The stride t_prev − t_curr is computed from Python floats and keeps full precision. But the time handed to the network is cast first:

t_vec = torch.full((B,), t_curr, dtype=img.dtype) and img.dtype is bfloat16

bfloat16 carries an 8-bit significand, so between 0.5 and 1.0 the representable times are spaced 2−8 = 0.00390625 apart. Near t = 1 the schedule's strides are much finer than that — at 2048×2048 with 50 steps the first stride is 0.0008 — so several consecutive rungs of the ladder land on the same bfloat16 number.

3.3 · What the step does not do

No clipping or renormalising The latent is never projected back onto a plausible range. Nothing stops an over-guided run from drifting; the guard is the guidance value itself (§6).
No added noise The trajectory is deterministic — an ODE solve, not an SDE. The only randomness in a generation is the initial randn.

§ 4Addresses: four numbers per token

Attention on its own cannot tell one token from another: shuffle the rows of the input and the output rows shuffle with them, unchanged. Something has to say where each row came from. FLUX.2 says it with four integers per token, and this file is what writes them.

x_ids : (B, N, 4) = (t, h, w, l) per token → 4 × 32 = 128 rotation numbers per head, inside the network

The four axes divide the work between them. Two of them describe a position inside a picture, one describes a position inside a prompt, and the fourth — t — says which plane a token belongs to. The canvas lives on plane 0. Every reference photograph gets its own plane, ten apart.

4.1 · Who writes which address

All three are built the same way: a dictionary of four 1-D coordinate ranges, fed to torch.cartesian_prod, which enumerates every combination with the last axis varying fastest. That ordering is not a detail — it is what makes the addresses line up with the tokens. The image branch flattens its latent grid with rearrange(x, "c h w -> (h w) c"), which also runs w fastest, so row i of the token tensor and row i of the id tensor describe the same patch of the picture. Text needs no such argument: its tokens are already a line, and its ids are arange(L) in the same order.

▸ Why four axes and not two

A two-axis address (row, column) would be enough for the canvas, and a one-axis address would be enough for the prompt — but the network attends over all of them at once, in a single sequence, so they need one shared coordinate system in which no two tokens accidentally look like the same place. Four axes give that: text is pinned to the origin of h and w and spreads along l; the canvas is pinned to the origin of l and spreads over h and w; and each reference gets a plane of its own along t. The network's positional encoder is told to spend 32 of its 128 numbers on each axis, and that budget is fixed — model.py refuses to build if sum(axes_dim) != head_dim.

4.2 · Why the references get t = 10, 20, 30

RoPE — the positional encoding FLUX.2 uses — turns each address into rotations, and rotations only ever depend on differences. So what the network actually feels is not "plane 10" but "ten units away from the canvas, and ten units away from the next reference". Two consequences follow, and both are why scale = 10 is a sensible constant:

No reference is privileged. Consecutive photos are all exactly ten apart from each other, so the model has no positional reason to prefer the first one it was given over the fourth. Had they been placed at t = 1, 2, 3, the gaps would still be equal — but the distance from the canvas would have been almost nothing, and reference one would have been nearly indistinguishable from it.

The canvas stays alone at t = 0. Ten units of separation is far outside anything the canvas produces internally (its own tokens differ only in h and w, all with t = 0), so "is this token part of the picture I am painting?" has an unambiguous positional answer.

✦ The t axis was free, so references took it inference

Nothing else in FLUX.2 uses the t axis: the canvas is always at 0 and text is always at 0, so during a normal generation all 128 numbers the network spends on that axis encode the constant zero. The t in the name suggests it was designed for something temporal — video frames, perhaps — and reference images are the thing that actually occupies it today. The file gives no comment either way; what is certain is that scale = 10 appears once, at the top of encode_image_refs, and is used nowhere else.

⚠ Addresses are assigned once, before the loop

x_ids, ctx_ids and ref_ids are computed outside the sampling loop and reused at every step — the geometry never changes, only the content of the tokens does. That is what makes the cached path of §15 possible: the reference tokens' keys and values depend on their addresses and on the timestep, and if the addresses are frozen and the timestep is frozen with them, the whole computation can be kept from step 0 and replayed.

§ 5Reference images: from JPEG to tokens

FLUX.2 edits and re-uses photographs: you hand it one or more images and it keeps what is in them while it paints something new. Those photographs have to become rows of 128 numbers with addresses before the network can see them, and getting there takes six functions and one hard-coded constant that is very nearly a typo.

5.1 · The preparation chain

Every reference photo goes through the same five stages, in this order, before it is encoded. The order is not arbitrary: each stage exists to make the next one safe.

StageWhat it guarantees for the next one
to_rgb Exactly three channels. A PNG with an alpha channel, or a greyscale scan, would otherwise reach ToTensor and produce a tensor the autoencoder cannot accept.
cap_min_pixels Nothing degenerate gets through: a side shorter than 64 pixels or an aspect ratio beyond 8:1 raises ValueError immediately. This is the only place in the file that refuses work rather than doing it.
cap_pixels A ceiling on the token count, and therefore on memory and on attention cost — which grows with the square of the sequence. It scales by sqrt(k / pixels) so the aspect ratio survives, and resamples with LANCZOS.
center_crop_to_multiple_of_x(16) Both sides divisible by 16, so the 8× autoencoder and its 2×2 patchify land on whole cells. It floors to the multiple and crops from the centre, taking half the remainder off each side.
default_images_prep A float tensor in [−1, 1]: ToTensor divides by 255, then 2·x − 1 recentres it. That is the range the autoencoder was trained on.

Cap first, then crop: the cap leaves a size that is almost a multiple of 16, and the crop removes the remainder — four pixels off each side in the example above. Reverse the two and the crop would be undone by the resize.

5.2 · The budget: 2024², not 2048²

How big a photo is allowed to be depends on how many photos there are, and the two numbers in the file are not the numbers you would expect:

one reference → limit_pixels = 20242 = 4 096 576 two or more → 10242 = 1 048 576 each
⚠ A 2.33 % typo, and exactly what it costs

The single-reference budget is 2024**2. The number that would make the design round — and that matches the 2048-pixel canvas the rest of FLUX.2 is documented for — is 2048**2: 4 194 304 pixels, 97 728 more, a 2.33 % difference. Reading a four-digit year where a power of two belongs is an easy mistake, and nothing else in the file uses 2024. inference

The consequence is concrete rather than cosmetic. A 2048×2048 reference is capped to 2024×2024, which is not a multiple of 16, so it is then cropped to 2016×2016 = 126×126 = 15 876 tokens. With a 2048² budget the crop would never fire and the same photo would give 128×128 = 16 384 tokens. One transposed digit removes 508 tokens — a 3 % strip around two edges of every single-reference edit. VERIFIED by running both stages on a 2048×2048 input.

The multi-reference budget is a different shape of decision: rather than letting four photos add up to four times the tokens, each photo is held to 1024² so that the total stays near one canvas' worth. The trade-off is per-photo detail — a 2048×2048 reference loses three quarters of its pixels when it is one of four, and none when it is alone.

5.3 · Two details that only the code reveals

Encoding happens on the GPU, unconditionally. The encode call is ae.encode(img[None].cuda()) — the .cuda() is written into the line, with no device argument and no fallback. The reference path therefore cannot run on a CPU-only or Apple-silicon setup even if the rest of the pipeline could. The [None] adds the batch axis the autoencoder expects, and the [0] immediately after the call takes it back off: what encode returns is one tensor of shape (1, 128, H/16, W/16), and prc_img wants (128, h, w).

Photos are encoded one at a time. There is no batching across references — a Python for loop calls the autoencoder once per photo. With four references that is four separate encoder passes, which is also why they can be different sizes: a batch would have to be padded to a common shape.

▸ Why 16, and not 8

The autoencoder alone downsamples by 8 in each direction. FLUX.2 then folds each 2×2 block of latent cells into one token, which is another factor of 2 per side. A side length divisible by 16 is what makes both steps come out whole: 2016 → 252 latent cells → 126 tokens per side. The crop is the cheapest place to enforce that, because it happens before any of the expensive work.

§ 6Two ways to be guided

The word "guidance" appears in every signature in this file, and it means two completely different things depending on which checkpoint you loaded. One is a number the network was trained to obey; the other is a subtraction performed outside the network. Confusing them is the easiest way to misread the three loops.

6.1 · Route A — guidance as an input

A guidance-distilled model has learned to take a strength dial as part of its input. The loop builds a tensor filled with that one float, hands it over with every call, and the network embeds it exactly the way it embeds the timestep: a 256-number sinusoidal code, a two-layer MLP, and a vector added to the timestep vector that steers every block. One batch, one call per step, no arithmetic afterwards.

pred = model(x, t, ctx, guidance=w) and that is the whole of it — nothing is combined or extrapolated

6.2 · Route B — guidance as an extrapolation

An ordinary model has no dial. To make it obey a prompt more strongly you run it twice per step — once on the real prompt and once on an empty one — and push the difference further than the model itself would:

pred = pred_uncond + w·(pred_cond − pred_uncond)

At w = 1 this returns the conditional prediction untouched. At w = 0 it returns the unconditional one — what the model would paint with no prompt at all. FLUX.2's non-distilled models ship with w = 4, which means going four times as far from "no prompt" as the network suggested, in the direction of "your prompt".

§ 7Caching at the loop level

The third loop exists for one arithmetic fact: with reference images, most of what the network looks at does not change between steps. Recomputing it fifty times is the single largest waste in a FLUX.2 edit — and removing it changes the mathematics, which is why it needs its own checkpoint.

7.1 · What is actually constant

Take one reference photograph and a 512×512 canvas. The image-side sequence the network attends over is 15 876 reference tokens plus 1 024 canvas tokens: 94 % of it is the photograph. Now ask what changes from step to step:

QuantityBetween two consecutive steps
Reference token contentIdentical. The encoded photo never changes during a generation.
Reference token addressesIdentical. ref_ids is built once, before the loop.
Reference tokens' timestep in the cached pathIdentical — and not merely in practice: forward_kv_extract gives the references a fixed timestep of ref_fixed_timestep = 0.0, and denoise_cached never overrides it. To the network, a reference is always a finished image, never a noisy one.
Reference tokens' timestep in the plain pathChanges. In denoise the references are simply concatenated to the canvas and the whole sequence is processed at the current t, so their modulation — and therefore their keys and values — is different at every step.
Canvas tokensChange every step. That is the point of the loop.

So in the plain path nothing can be cached: the references really are recomputed, and they really do produce different keys and values each time. Caching is not an optimisation of denoise — it is a different model of the world in which the references are frozen at t = 0 and their contribution is computed once.

7.2 · What the cache costs

Keys and values are not free. Every block stores two tensors of hidden_size numbers per cached token, in bfloat16 — so four bytes per token per block for K and V together:

It costs one table of size max + 1 and two gather operations, and it has no Python loop in it — which matters, because this runs on tensors that can be tens of thousands of elements long.

∑ The two ends are safe by construction

The expression divides by t, and the schedule always contains t = 1 and t = 0. Neither breaks it. At t = 1 the term (1/t − 1) is exactly 0, so the result is eμ/(eμ+0) = 1. At t = 0 the same term is +inf in IEEE floating point, and eμ/(eμ+inf) = 0 — no exception, no NaN, just the right answer. The endpoints are fixed points of the map for every μ, which is what lets the ladder start at pure noise and end at a finished image no matter how hard it is bent.

Each reference token is 128 numbers because that is what the autoencoder emits per patch: 32 latent channels with each 2×2 block of cells folded into the channel dimension, 32 × 2 × 2 = 128. 14.1 · What the model actually sees

Because no num_ref_tokens is passed, the references are not "conditioning" in any structural sense — they are extra image tokens. They attend to the canvas, the canvas attends to them, the text attends to both, and all of them are modulated by the same timestep vector. A reference photograph, which is a perfectly clean image, is processed as though it were as noisy as the half-finished canvas it is guiding.

14.2 · Three details worth knowing

The slice is unconditional in practice. Line 302 tests if img_input_ids is not None — but img_input_ids is img_ids, which the caller always provides, so the test always passes. Without references the slice is a no-op (the prediction is already exactly img.shape[1] wide); with them it discards 15 876 columns of computed predictions. The guard reads as if it were protecting against a missing-ids case that the signature does not allow.

The concatenation is rebuilt every step. torch.cat on lines 292–293 allocates a fresh 16 900-token tensor at each of the N iterations, copying the references every time. Since neither the reference tokens nor their ids change, that copy is pure overhead — small next to the transformer call, but not zero: at 512² with one reference it is 4.3 MB per step in bfloat16.

The model returns more than the sampler wants. Flux2.forward strips the text tokens but not the reference tokens, so the sampler has to. The cached path is the opposite: forward_kv_extract strips both before its final layer, which is why denoise_cached contains no slicing at all.

▸ Why the ordinary forward pass builds a cache it throws away

In model.py, Flux2.forward runs its blocks through forward_kv_extract with num_ref_tokens=0 and assigns the returned cache to _. One code path serves both modes: with zero reference tokens the "extract" is a no-op that costs a few tensor slices, and the model does not need a second implementation of its own forward. The sampler never sees any of this — it just calls model(...).

✦ Why step 0 is allowed to be expensive inference

The first step of the cached loop is more work than a step of the plain loop: it processes the references, the canvas and the text, and it stores K and V for every block on the way. The saving only starts at step 1. With four steps that is one expensive step and three cheap ones — which is why the published speedups are modest at four steps (1.78× with one reference) and why the trick is worth much more at fifty. The measured numbers, from the repository's own benchmark document, are 1.78× and 2.66× at 512², and 1.21× and 1.85× at 1440², where the canvas is big enough that the references matter less.

16.1 · Why the halves stay in step

After the combine, both halves of img hold the same numbers: line 406 duplicates the guided prediction, and line 408 adds it to both. From that point the two halves are identical, and they stay identical for the rest of the generation — the only thing that differs between them is the text they are paired with, which the network sees but the image does not.

So the second half is redundant state. It costs a tensor copy per step and a doubled batch in the next call, which is not redundant at all: that call needs both halves, because guidance is a difference between two predictions and neither one can be computed without running its prompt. The alternative — keeping one image and duplicating it inside the loop — would allocate the same doubled tensor every step instead of once. inference

▸ The order of the halves is a contract, and it is written in a comment

pred_uncond, pred_cond = pred.chunk(2) assumes the unconditional prediction is in row 0. Nothing in this function enforces that; what enforces it is the caller, which builds ctx = torch.cat([ctx_empty, ctx_prompt], dim=0) — empty first. Swap the two there and every generation would be extrapolated in the wrong direction, away from the prompt, with no error and no exception. The comment on line 368 exists to stop exactly that.

▸ What the seed does and does not control

torch.randn with a seeded generator is the only random act in a generation. The schedule is a deterministic function of the step count and the canvas size; the reference tokens are a deterministic function of the photographs; the loop is pure arithmetic. The same seed, prompt, canvas and reference give the same image — but changing the canvas size changes μ, which changes the ladder, which changes the result even at the same seed.

§ 18Why it looks like this

Twelve decisions, visible in the code, each with the line that shows it. Some are excellent, some are consequences of history, and four of them are code that does nothing.

DecisionWhereWhat it buys, and what it costs
No classeswhole file There is no sampler to construct, configure or keep alive; the entire state of a generation is the img tensor and a Python loop counter. It costs a little repetition — three loops each rebuild a guidance vector and a time tensor — and it buys the ability to read any function in isolation.
Three loops instead of one269, 310, 364 Each loop is a straight line with no branches except the ones it is about. A single loop with use_cache and use_cfg flags would be shorter and much harder to trust. The Euler step is written three times as a result.
Ids threaded everywhere24, 93, 141 Position is data, not a property of the tensor, so a reference can be moved to another plane without touching its content — and the same flat token list can be turned back into a picture after the loop (scatter_ids). The cost is that every function has twice as many arguments.
Two wrappers, not four addressers106, 122 "Per sample" and "as a collection" are separated, so the batch/list distinction is one line each and the address logic exists once per modality.
PIL at the edges, tensors inside226–237 Cropping, resampling and aspect checks are done by PIL, which is good at them; the tensor world starts at ToTensor. Nothing in between: there is no numpy stage.
Validation raises, everything else repairs199–202 A too-thin or too-extreme image is a mistake by the caller, so it is reported; a too-large image is normal, so it is resized. One function in the file says no.
Constants inline53, 56, 58, 227, 252–253, 255 Eight fitted decimals, three pixel budgets, a plane spacing of 10, a knee at 4 300, a multiple of 16, an aspect limit of 8 and a minimum side of 64 — none of them in a config, none of them named. Reading the file tells you everything; changing any of it means editing the function.
.cuda() written in72 The reference path assumes a CUDA device with no argument and no fallback, while the loops themselves are device-agnostic — they inherit whatever device the tensors are on.
The schedule becomes a Python list248 Slicing ([:-1], [1:]) and zip then cost nothing and need no device, and the stride arithmetic keeps full float precision instead of the image's bfloat16 (§3.2).
Guidance always passed283, 398 The loops do not ask whether the model has a guidance embedding; they always build the tensor and let model.py decide. Uniform code, and a silently ignored argument for four of the six checkpoints.
The batch stays doubled in CFG406, 408, 410 One duplication at the top, one at the end, and a stable shape through the loop — at the price of carrying an identical second half for the whole generation.
Nothing is loggedwhole file No progress bar, no per-step timing, no warnings. The script prints; this file computes.
