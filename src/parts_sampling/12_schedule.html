§ 2The schedule: which times to ask about

A generation cannot visit every moment between pure noise and a finished image — it can afford four visits, or fifty. Which four? FLUX.2's answer is the most consequential number-crunching in this file: three small functions, eleven hard-coded constants, and a ladder of times that is deliberately, strongly uneven.

2.1 · The obvious ladder, and why it is not used

The obvious choice is evenly spaced: eight steps means t = 1.000, 0.875, 0.750, 0.625, 0.500, 0.375, 0.250, 0.125, and then 0. Every step covers exactly one eighth of the way. FLUX.2 does not do this. With the same eight steps, at 1024×1024, it asks about:

t = 1.000, 0.985, 0.967, 0.942, 0.906, 0.853, 0.763, 0.580 → 0

Four of the eight calls happen while the image is still more than 94 % noise, and the very last step crosses 58 % of the entire range in one go — from t = 0.580 straight to t = 0. That looks wasteful until you notice what the two schedules are spending their steps on. The even ladder spends half its calls in the late phase, where the picture is already formed and each call only refines texture. The bent ladder spends nearly all of them in the early phase, where composition, lighting and shapes are decided — and then trusts one long stride to finish the job.

2.2 · Bending the ladder: one number, applied to all of it

The bending is done by a single formula, and it is worth understanding it exactly, because it is the only place where "how many steps" and "how big the image" reach the pixels. Take any time t between 0 and 1 and map it to a new time t′:

t′ = eμeμ + (1t − 1)σ with σ = 1 in FLUX.2, so the exponent does nothing

Read literally, this is a signal-to-noise ratio being rescaled: (1/t − 1) is the ratio of noise to signal at time t, and dividing it by eμ makes every moment look noisier than it was. But there is a much better way to see it.

∑ The shift is a slide along the logit axis

Define the logit of a time as logit(t) = ln(t / (1 − t)) — the logarithm of the signal-to-noise ratio. It runs from −∞ at t = 0 through 0 at t = 0.5 to +∞ at t = 1. Now rewrite the formula with σ = 1:

t′ = eμ·teμ·t + (1 − t) ⇒ t′1 − t′ = eμ·t1 − t ⇒ logit(t′) = logit(t) + μ

That is the whole trick: the shift adds a constant to the logit of every timestep. With the general σ it is the affine map logit(t′) = σ·logit(t) + μ — a stretch and a slide. The two ends are fixed points (±∞ + μ = ±∞), which is why t = 1 and t = 0 survive any shift, and why μ can be large without breaking the ladder. ▸ What "noisier" buys you

The published reason for shifting is resolution. At a fixed t, a bigger image hides its noise better: with four times as many latent cells, averaging over neighbours recovers more of the original signal, so the same t is an easier problem. If you keep the ladder fixed while the canvas grows, the model spends its steps on questions it has already answered. Shifting by a larger μ at larger resolutions restores the difficulty — which is exactly the dependence on image_seq_len that compute_empirical_mu implements (§2.3). This argument and the shift itself come from the Stable Diffusion 3 paper (Esser et al., 2024, §5.2).

2.3 · Where the bend comes from: two fitted lines

So the shift is one number μ. Where does μ come from? Not from a config file and not from a formula derived from first principles — from two straight lines fitted to measurements, plus a linear interpolation between them. Call L the number of image tokens in the canvas (4 096 at 1024×1024) and N the number of steps:

m10(L) = 8.73809524·10−5·L + 1.89833333 the μ that suits a 10-step run
m200(L) = 1.6927·10−4·L + 0.45666666 the μ that suits a 200-step run

For a canvas of L ≤ 4 300 tokens the code interpolates between those two anchors on the step count, and it does it so that both ends come out exactly right: with a = (m₂₀₀ − m₁₀) / 190 and b = m₂₀₀ − 200a, the value μ = a·N + b equals m10 at N = 10 and m200 at N = 200. For a canvas above 4 300 tokens it skips the interpolation entirely and returns m200(L), whatever the step count.

Two things about this are worth stopping for.

Fewer steps means a stronger shift. Both fitted lines rise with L, but the 10-step line rises more slowly and starts higher, so in the whole supported range m10 > m200 and the slope a = (m₂₀₀ − m₁₀)/190 is negative: μ falls as the step count grows. A model with only four chances puts them all high up in the noise; a model with fifty can afford to spend the last twenty on detail. (The two lines would only cross at L ≈ 17 605 tokens, well past the 16 384 tokens of a 4-megapixel canvas, so this ordering never reverses in practice.)

⚠ The schedule is not continuous in resolution

At the knee, μ jumps. Ask for a 1040×1040 image and the canvas is 65×65 = 4 225 tokens, so a 4-step run interpolates and gets μ = 2.3021. Ask for 1056×1056 — 1.5 % more pixels — and the canvas is 66×66 = 4 356 tokens, past the knee, so the same 4-step run gets μ = 1.1940. The bend is nearly halved by a rounding boundary, and the ladder changes shape with it — [1.000, 0.908, 0.767, 0.524, 0] instead of [1.000, 0.968, 0.909, 0.769, 0], i.e. the model is suddenly asked about t = 0.524 where a moment ago it was asked about 0.769. Nothing in the file guards against this; a 50-step run over the same two sizes moves μ from 2.0368 to 1.1940 and its final stride from 0.135 to 0.063. VERIFIED by calling compute_empirical_mu and get_schedule at 4 225 and 4 356 tokens.

✦ What the constants were fitted to inference

The function is named compute_empirical_mu and its eight decimals are unmistakably measured rather than derived, but the repository does not say what was measured. The shape of the two lines suggests a sweep over canvas sizes and step counts, scoring the samples and keeping the μ that looked best at each end of the step range — the same recipe FLUX.1 used, where μ was interpolated between two sequence lengths. The knee at 4 300 tokens then reads as "above roughly one megapixel, the step count stopped mattering in the measurements". Treat that reading as a guess; the numbers themselves are not guesses.

Read down the last column and three habits of this sampler become visible.

The first call is always at exactly t = 1. The ladder starts at pure noise and the shift leaves 1 alone, so the network's first question is always "here is Gaussian noise, which way?" — a case it was trained on.

The model is never called at t = 0. The zero at the end of every ladder is a destination, not a query: the loop pairs each time with the next one and calls the network at the first of the pair, so the last entry is only ever used as a stride length. This is why the final step is so long, and why "asking the model what a finished image looks like" never happens.

Resolution moves the whole ladder, not just its length. At 50 steps and 1024² the model gets 39 of its 50 calls above t = 0.7; at 2048² it gets 46. The last stride grows from 0.1337 to 0.3403 — the higher the resolution, the more of the descent is left to one final jump.

✦ Reference images do not change the schedule

The step count and the canvas size are the only inputs to get_schedule, and the canvas size it is given is the token count of the noise — four reference photographs add 16 384 tokens to every call and leave the ladder untouched. The schedule is chosen by the picture you want, never by the pictures you brought along.
