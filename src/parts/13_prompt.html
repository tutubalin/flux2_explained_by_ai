§ 3From prompt to tokens — a 24-billion-parameter reader

The transformer's text input is 512 rows of 15 360 numbers. Not one word, not one embedding, not a sentence vector: 512 rows, each of them 15 360 wide. This chapter is where those two strange numbers come from — and the answer is that FLUX.2 reads your prompt with a large language model, then taps it at three depths and glues the results together.

3.1 · A language model that is never asked to speak

FLUX.2's text encoder is a decoder-only LLM: Mistral-Small-3.2-24B for the dev model, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-4B for the two klein checkpoints. It is loaded frozen, it is not fine-tuned, and it is not asked to generate a single token. The prompt goes in, the model runs one forward pass, and what comes out is not text — it is the model's internal state, the numbers it was carrying about your sentence while it read it.

That is a different job from the one a language model was trained for, and it is worth pausing on, because it explains the rest of this chapter. An LLM's final layer is optimised for one thing: predicting the next word. Its earlier layers are not. They hold the intermediate work — which words are near each other, what refers to what, what kind of sentence this is — and none of it was ever trained to be a description suitable for painting with. So FLUX.2 does not take the last layer. It takes three.

3.2 · Three depths, glued side by side

Three hidden layers are read out of the stack and concatenated along the feature axis — layers 10, 20 and 30 for Mistral, 9, 18 and 27 for Qwen3. Roughly the shallow third, the middle and the deep end: local and syntactic, then relational, then fully semantic. Each of those layers carries one vector per prompt token, and gluing three of them side by side makes each token three times as wide.

Klein 4B      : Qwen3-4B,              layers [ 9, 18, 27], hidden 2560 → 3 × 2560 = 7680  ✓

Three different language models, three different widths, one recipe: three depths times the hidden width. That is the whole explanation of the most mysterious-looking constant in the configuration table.

Reading mid-depth layers rather than only the last one is a trick with precedent — SD3 does something similar — and the reasoning is the one above: a next-word predictor's output layer is specialised for predicting words, and an image model wants everything the reader understood on the way to that prediction. inference The layer indices are in the source; the justification is not written down anywhere.

3.3 · 512 tokens, whatever you typed

The second strange number is a limit: MAX_LENGTH = 512. Prompts are tokenised and then padded or cut to exactly 512 tokens, so a three-word prompt and a paragraph both arrive as 512 rows. Two consequences, and both show up later in the arithmetic:

  • The text half of the sequence is a constant. Image tokens grow with resolution — 1 024 at 512², 4 096 at 1024², 16 384 at 2048² — while text tokens never move from 512. That is why quadrupling the canvas does not quite quadruple the work of attention (§21.2), and why reference images dominate the sequence so completely at small sizes (§20).
  • Padding rows are real rows. They are attended to like any other token; the network learns to ignore them. Nothing in model.py masks them out, because nothing needs to.
⚠ The word "token" means two different things in this article

A language-model token is a piece of a word — what the tokeniser counts, 512 of them. An image token is a 16 × 16 patch of the picture — what the VAE and the patchifier produce, 4 096 of them at 1024². They meet inside the transformer as rows of the same sequence, and from that point on the distinction is only in their position ids (§8). When this article says "tokens" without qualifying it, it means rows of the transformer's sequence.
