§ 10SiLUActivation — the soft switch

Between any two linear layers, a network needs something that is not linear, or the two layers collapse into one and the depth buys nothing. FLUX.2 uses exactly one such function, in two different jobs, and it has its own class in model.py. It is four lines long. This chapter is about the idea, which is older than the file and worth understanding on its own terms.

10.1 · The problem with a hard corner

The classic choice is ReLU: relu(x) = max(0, x). Negative inputs become exactly zero, positive ones pass through. It is cheap and it works, and it has one bad property: a unit whose input is negative has no gradient at all. During training, if a unit drifts into that region it stops learning — the famous "dead neuron" — and the capacity spent on it is lost. The corner at zero is also a discontinuity in the derivative, which makes the loss surface kinked in a way optimisers do not love.

The fix is to soften the corner. Instead of switching off abruptly, switch off gradually: use a smooth function that is near zero for very negative inputs and near one for very positive ones, and multiply the input by it. That smooth switch is the sigmoid σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e−x), and the result is SiLU — also called Swish:

Σ SiLU, in full

silu(x) = x · σ(x) = x / (1 + e−x)

smooth everywhere · no dead region · non-monotonic: it dips slightly negative before climbing

Read it as a sentence: a number, multiplied by its own smoothed on/off switch. Large positive inputs are essentially untouched (σ ≈ 1). Large negative inputs are essentially erased (σ ≈ 0) — but "essentially" is doing the work: they are multiplied by a tiny number rather than set to zero, so a gradient still flows and the unit can come back. And around x ≈ −1.28 the product dips to about −0.28 before recovering, which means the function is not simply "a soft ReLU": it can express a small negative response, something ReLU cannot do at all.

10.2 · Job one: an activation

The ordinary use. Anywhere the file needs a nonlinearity between two linear layers — the two-layer network that turns a timestep fingerprint into a condition vector (§15.3), and the projection that turns that vector into modulation dials (§15.9) — it puts SiLU in the middle. Two linear maps with nothing between them would compose into a single linear map, so all that depth would be wasted; one SiLU is what makes them two genuinely different transformations.

10.3 · Job two: a valve

The second use is more interesting, and it is why the class exists at all rather than being a call to torch.nn.functional.silu. Inside every one of the 56 blocks, the MLP projection is made twice as wide as it needs to be, and then split in half:

Σ SwiGLU — what the blocks' MLPs actually compute

mlp_out = silu(A) ⊙ B

A and B are the two halves of one wide projection · ⊙ is element-wise · one half decides how much of the other half gets through

Here SiLU is not shaping a signal, it is gating one. Half of the projection (A) is turned into a set of per-channel valves — mostly open, mostly shut, or anywhere between — and the other half (B) is the content that gets through them. The network learns, per token and per channel, how much of its own computation to let pass. This is the SwiGLU family of gated MLPs (Shazeer 2020, §25), and it is the same "multiply by a learned amount" idea that the modulation gate of §11.2 applies to whole sub-layers — one at channel level, one at block level.

It is also why the class is a module and not a function: the blocks hold it as self.mlp_act, so it appears in the module tree, and it is why the MLP widths in the configuration are not what they seem — a "3×" MLP ratio produces a projection that is split, so the effective hidden width is 3× while the matrix is 6× wide (§15.2 has the arithmetic).

◇ The one-line summary

SiLU is x times a smooth switch that depends on x itself. Used between two linear layers it makes depth meaningful; used against a second copy of the signal it becomes a per-channel valve. FLUX.2 does both, with the same four lines, in 58 places.
