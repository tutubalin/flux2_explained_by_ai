§ 2From pixels to tokens: what the model actually eats

model.py has no convolutions and no image logic. It receives images that have already been flattened into a list of vectors. To understand its shapes you must know exactly how that flattening happens.

2.1 · Three stages of compression

Two of the three stages happen inside the VAE, in autoencoder.py's encode(): the convolutional 8× downsample to 32 channels, and the 2×2 patchify that folds four latent cells into one 128-number token. The third — flattening that 2-D grid of tokens into a plain sequence and attaching the position ids — is sampling.py's prc_img, run just before the model is called:

◇ Beginner · why bother with latents at all?

Attention costs grow with the square of the token count. A 1024×1024 image as raw pixels would be over a million tokens: 10¹² attention pairs per layer, utterly impossible. As 4096 tokens it is 21 million pairs — expensive but feasible on real hardware. The latent space is a compression scheme that throws away perceptually irrelevant detail (exact sub-pixel noise) while keeping everything a human would notice. One detail worth knowing, because it explains a shape you will see in autoencoder.py: the 128-channel latent is normalised by a running-statistics BatchNorm2d(128, affine=False, eps=1e-4) inside the autoencoder (autoencoder.py L293–325) — no per-channel scale/shift factors for the transformer to carry around.

Quadrupling the pixels quadruples the image tokens — 4096 at 1 MP, 16 384 at 4 MP. The 512 text tokens do not grow, so the joint sequence goes 4608 → 16 896, a factor of 3.7, and attention work, which is quadratic in that joint length, by 13.4×. That single fact explains most of FLUX.2's engineering: the KV cache for references (§15), the shared modulation (§17), and the choice of 48 cheap single-stream blocks over expensive double-stream ones.

2.3 · Text tokens, and the mystery of 15360

The text side is simpler but has one surprise. The 24-billion-parameter Mistral VLM is run over your prompt, and then three of its internal layers — layers 10, 20 and 30 — are pulled out and stacked side by side:

OUTPUT_LAYERS_MISTRAL = [10, 20, 30] # which VLM layers to read out = torch.stack([output.hidden_states[k] for k in OUTPUT_LAYERS_MISTRAL], dim=1) return rearrange(out, "b c l d -> b l (c d)") # (B, 512, 3*5120) = (B, 512, 15360)

Mistral-Small-3.2 has a hidden width of 5120, so each prompt token arrives as 3 × 5120 = 15360 numbers. That is precisely Flux2Params.context_in_dim. And the reason the Klein configs use 12288 and 7680 is exactly the same trick with different teachers: Qwen3-8B (4096 × 3) and Qwen3-4B (2560 × 3). Using mid-depth layers rather than only the last one gives the flow transformer access to both syntactic and semantic features — a trick also used in SD3.

Prompts are padded and truncated to MAX_LENGTH = 512 tokens, which is where the "512 text tokens" column above comes from.

2.4 · Reference images are just more tokens

For editing and multi-reference generation, reference images go through the same VAE encoder and the same patchify, and their tokens are simply appended to the sequence. Up to ten references are supported; to keep the cost sane the pipeline caps reference pixels at 1024² each when there are several, and 2024² when there is only one (sampling.py L56–58 spells it 2024**2, which is what upstream says — almost certainly a typo for 2048²). The only thing that distinguishes a reference token from a canvas token is its position id — which is the subject of §4.
