§ 5Attention, from zero

Everything from here on is built from one operation: attention. Every paper mentions it; few explain it. We will build it from scratch using nothing but a dot product and one paragraph about softmax — and by the end you will know exactly what a query, a key and a value are, why scores are divided by √d, what a "head" is, and, crucially for everything that follows, what attention cannot see.

5.1 · The question every token must answer

A token on its own knows almost nothing. The canvas token at row 17, column 42 holds a small patch of half-finished image; whether that patch should become "fox ear" or "shadow on snow" depends on tokens far away from it — the word fox in the prompt, a patch of the reference image showing the actual animal. So at every layer of the network, every token needs to ask the same question:

Σ The question

"Everybody else — what do you have that I need?"

Attention is the machinery that answers this question for all tokens at once, in parallel, using nothing but matrix multiplications. It is a soft database lookup: instead of fetching one record, every token takes a weighted slice of everything.

5.2 · Three roles per token: query, key, value

Each token vector x is linearly projected into three new vectors — three roles in the lookup:

  • the query q — the question this token is asking: what I am looking for;
  • the key k — the advertisement this token broadcasts: what I have on offer;
  • the value v — the payload this token will actually hand over if it gets chosen: what I carry.

A conference is the right picture: your query is what you hope to find, someone's key is what their name badge says, their value is the folder under their arm. How well a question matches an advertisement is measured by the simplest thing available — the dot product q·k: large when the two vectors point the same way, near zero when they are unrelated, negative when they oppose. The three projections are learned matrices (in FLUX.2 they are fused into a single matmul — the SelfAttention bundle of §11.8); nothing about them is hand-designed.

5.3 · From scores to weights: divide by √d, then softmax

Raw dot products need two adjustments before they can be used as weights.

First, scale. A dot product of two d-dimensional vectors adds d terms, so its typical size grows like √d — with d = 128 (the width of one head's q and k in FLUX.2, §10) that is a factor of ~11 of pure size, carrying no information at all. Dividing every score by √d removes it and keeps the next step sane.

Second, softmax. Weights must be positive and sum to one, so that "taking a little of everyone" is a well-defined mix. Softmax does exactly that: exponentiate each score (now everything is positive, and bigger scores are amplified), then divide by the total:

wᵢ = esᵢΣⱼ esⱼ with sᵢ = q · kᵢ√d and the answer is out = Σ wᵢ · v

Because exponentiation is steep, a score that is merely a bit larger than the others can take most of the weight — softmax is a soft maximum: usually "mostly one, a little of the rest", never a hard cut. (Which entries are allowed to compete at all is a separate question — FLUX.2's mask — and gets its own section, §12.)

5.4 · Heads: many lookups in parallel

One lookup can encode one kind of relationship at a time — "looks like", "sits next to", "contradicts". Real language and real images need dozens of such relationships at once, so the feature width is sliced into H independent sub-lookups called heads: FLUX.2 [dev] splits its 6144 dimensions into 48 heads of 128 dimensions each (§10), and every head runs its own private copy of §5.3 on its own slice — its own q, k, v, its own scores, its own weights. The head outputs are concatenated back into one vector and mixed by a final projection. Nothing in the code coordinates the heads — the 48 slices are just 48 independent lookups that happen to be computed in one batched matmul — and what each head ends up specialising in is a property of the trained weights, not of this file.

Remember the number 128: it is head_dim, the width of one head's query and key — and therefore the size of everything in this section. It appears again in the norms (§11.7), in the rotation planes (§6) and in every parameter count in this article.

5.5 · What attention cannot see: order

Notice what is missing from all of the above: position. The weights depend only on the contents of the vectors. Shuffle the tokens and the weights shuffle with them — the set of outputs comes back identical, rearranged. Attention is a perfect lookup and a total amnesiac: left to itself it has no idea which patches are neighbours, which reference image a patch came from, or which word preceded which in your prompt.

Everything spatial and sequential must therefore be injected into q and k before the dot product is taken. That is precisely the job of the next section — and the reason FLUX.2's position system is worth a chapter of its own.
